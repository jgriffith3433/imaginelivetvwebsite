# The Water Road: voice

A hero origin in the Old West: a water caravan, a dry town, a revolver you never fire well. You win by giving,
never by taking and never by the gun. Plain English, spoken by one narrator over a drifting game camera.

## Tone
Dry, warm and a little wry, like someone telling it years later on a porch. Hard country, kind ending. One image
that sticks per page ("green as a lie", "loud as church bells"), then stop.

## Pacing
Two sentences of story, one question of four to six words. Water is the clock: nearly every page costs a drop,
so every page should have a reason to feel thirst. Deaths are fast and quiet; the endings take one breath more.

## Choice design
Choices are the moral fork in the page: give or keep, wait or push on, talk or shoot. The kind choice is never
the safe-looking one, and the cruel one is never punished by a trick: it just costs you the way you were seen.
Three choices only where there is a real third way (the dry wash).

## Style note: 5 rules
1. **Plain words, American idiom.** "Mules", "bung", "dipper", "caravan". No archaisms that need a glossary.
2. **One simile per page, from the work or the land.** "Shine like a stove top." Never from a film or a book.
3. **Cost first.** Each page says what the desert took or what it will cost before it says what you feel.
4. **The dove is never named.** No page says bird, dove, wings or feather (`unspoken` in the data); it lives in the pictures only.
5. **Choices are things you can do with your hands, 3 to 7 words, and the moral one reads as plainly as the selfish one.** ("Give them your flask" / "Tell them the water's spoken for".)

## Canon the words keep
Forty barrels, six wagons, Dry Fork; the trail boss; the boy with the spyglass and his family; the five riders;
the spring on nobody's map. Two endings: *Dry Fork* (the hero) and *The price of water*.
